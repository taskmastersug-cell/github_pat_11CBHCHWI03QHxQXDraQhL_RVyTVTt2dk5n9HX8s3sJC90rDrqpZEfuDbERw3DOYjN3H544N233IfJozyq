How is Your Due Date Actually Calculated?

The most widely used method for estimating a due date is Naegele's Rule, developed by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele in the early 1800s. The rule is elegantly simple: take the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP), subtract three months, and add seven days — the result is your estimated due date (EDD), roughly 280 days (40 weeks) after your LMP.

This works because Naegele's Rule assumes a textbook 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation occurring on day 14. In reality, very few people have a perfectly average cycle. If your cycle is longer than 28 days, ovulation (and conception) happens later — so the true due date is pushed back. Shorter cycles pull the EDD forward. Our calculator automatically applies this adjustment using the formula:

Adjusted EDD = LMP + 280 days + (Your Cycle Length − 28)

Gestational Age vs. Fertilization Age

These two terms are routinely confused. Gestational age is counted from the first day of your LMP — even though you weren't technically pregnant yet at that point. Fertilization (or fetal) age begins at conception, which usually happens about two weeks after the LMP. The result: when your doctor says you are "10 weeks pregnant," the embryo itself is roughly 8 weeks old.

Why Cycle Length Matters

Studies show that fewer than 5% of pregnant individuals deliver on their predicted due date. The single biggest driver of inaccuracy is variation in cycle length. Someone with a 35-day cycle who relies on a standard 28-day calculator can be a full week off — enough to affect screening windows, scheduled inductions, and even decisions around viability. Adjusting for your real cycle gives you a meaningfully more precise window.

The Comprehensive Trimester Timeline

Pregnancy unfolds in three distinct phases, each with its own physiological milestones, common symptoms, and recommended medical scans. Use the timeline below to know what to expect — and when to ask questions.

Trimester 1 — Weeks 1 to 12: The Foundation

Weeks 1–4: Conception & Implantation

  • Ovulation and fertilization typically occur around week 2.
  • The blastocyst implants in the uterine lining around week 4.
  • A missed period is usually the earliest sign.

Weeks 5–8: Rapid Embryonic Development

  • The neural tube, heart, and limb buds form.
  • Common symptoms: morning sickness, fatigue, breast tenderness.
  • First prenatal appointment often scheduled around week 8.

Weeks 9–12: Fetal Period Begins

  • The embryo is now officially a fetus.
  • Dating ultrasound typically performed between weeks 8 and 13.
  • Optional NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing) available from week 10.

Trimester 2 — Weeks 13 to 26: The Golden Period

Weeks 13–16: Energy Returns

  • Nausea typically subsides; appetite returns.
  • A small baby bump may become visible.

Weeks 17–20: Anatomy Scan

  • Mid-pregnancy ultrasound evaluates organ development.
  • Many people feel quickening — the first fetal movements.
  • Biological sex can usually be identified.

Weeks 21–26: Steady Growth

  • Glucose screening for gestational diabetes around weeks 24–28.
  • Baby develops sleep cycles and responds to sound.
  • Threshold of viability is generally reached at week 24.

Trimester 3 — Weeks 27 to 40+: The Home Stretch

Weeks 27–32: Preparing the Lungs

  • Lungs begin producing surfactant.
  • Symptoms intensify: heartburn, swelling, shortness of breath.
  • Tdap vaccine typically administered weeks 27–36.

Weeks 33–36: Final Positioning

  • Most babies turn head-down.
  • Group B Strep (GBS) test around weeks 36–37.
  • Hospital bag and birth plan finalized.

Weeks 37–40+: Full Term

  • Early term at 37 weeks; full term at 39 weeks.
  • Watch for signs of labor: contractions, mucus plug, water breaking.
  • Post-term begins at 42 weeks — induction usually discussed by 41 weeks.

Pregnancy Due Date FAQs

Quick answers to the questions expectant parents ask most.

How accurate is an online pregnancy due date calculator?
An LMP-based calculator that adjusts for cycle length is accurate within a few days for most people. Only about 4% of babies arrive on the exact estimated due date; the vast majority are born within two weeks before or after. A first-trimester ultrasound dating scan remains the most precise method.
Can I use this calculator if my cycle is irregular?
You can, but the estimate will be less reliable. Enter the average length of your cycle. If your cycles vary significantly, an early ultrasound (between weeks 8 and 13) provides the most accurate dating.
What is the difference between gestational age and fetal age?
Gestational age is measured from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP). Fetal (or fertilization) age is measured from the day of conception, which typically happens about two weeks later. Gestational age is therefore roughly two weeks longer than fetal age.
Can my due date change during pregnancy?
Yes. If a first-trimester ultrasound measurement of the embryo differs from the LMP-based date by more than about 7 days, clinicians usually adjust the due date to the ultrasound estimate.
What if I conceived through IVF?
IVF allows extremely precise dating. The due date is calculated from the embryo transfer date + 266 days, minus the age of the embryo at transfer (e.g., 3 or 5 days). For IVF pregnancies, do not rely on an LMP calculator.
When is the best time to take a pregnancy test?
Most home pregnancy tests are reliable from the first day of a missed period. Testing earlier may produce a false negative because hCG levels are still too low to detect.
